Allergic contact dermatitis: causes, symptoms, treatment and precautions
Source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000869.htm

Contact dermatitis is skin inflammation caused by touching a substance that irritates the skin or triggers an allergy. Irritant contact dermatitis is the most common type and results from direct damage, for example from soaps, solvents or prolonged moisture. Allergic contact dermatitis occurs when the immune system becomes sensitized to a substance such as nickel, latex, fragrances, hair dyes, adhesives, certain topical medicines or plants such as poison ivy. It is not contagious.

The rash is confined to the area that touched the substance, so the shape often gives a clue: sharp borders, straight lines or the outline of a bracelet or glove. Typical symptoms are itching, redness, dry cracked skin and, in allergic cases, small blisters and swelling. Burning and pain are more typical of the irritant form. Repeated exposure can make the skin thick and chronically inflamed.

Treatment has two parts: identify and avoid the cause, and calm the inflammation. Wash the skin after contact, use cool compresses and emollients, and protect the hands with suitable gloves. Topical corticosteroids are the usual medicine (1% hydrocortisone for the face and folds, medium potency for the trunk and limbs). Oral antihistamines such as cetirizine relieve itching. Severe, widespread reactions may need a short course of oral steroids. Patch testing can identify the allergen.

Precautions: do not apply corticosteroids to untreated skin infections. Medicines themselves, including steroid creams and propylene glycol in some products, can cause contact allergy; if a rash worsens after applying a product, stop it and seek advice. Most cases clear in two to three weeks if the trigger is avoided.
