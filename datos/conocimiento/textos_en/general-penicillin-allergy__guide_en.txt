Penicillin allergy: how to recognize it and which antibiotics to avoid
Source: https://www.mayoclinic.org/es/diseases-conditions/penicillin-allergy/symptoms-causes/syc-20376222; https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/penicillin-allergy.htm; https://www.msdmanuals.com/es/professional (Manual MSD, cefalosporinas); https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/hcp/clinical-signs/index.html

Penicillin allergy is an immune reaction to this family of antibiotics. Symptoms may appear minutes to hours after a dose and include hives, itching, swelling of the lips, face or tongue, wheezing, difficulty breathing, vomiting and, in severe cases, anaphylaxis, an emergency with a drop in blood pressure and trouble breathing. Delayed reactions, such as a rash appearing days later, and rare severe skin reactions also occur.

Penicillin-family antibiotics to avoid in a confirmed allergy include penicillin V, amoxicillin, ampicillin, dicloxacillin, oxacillin and amoxicillin with clavulanic acid.

Cephalosporins share some chemical similarity with penicillins, but the risk of cross-reaction is low, especially with newer generations. Cephalexin, a first-generation drug, shares side chains with amoxicillin and ampicillin and is used with more caution; it is contraindicated in patients with cephalosporin allergy or prior anaphylaxis to penicillins.

Common alternatives when beta-lactams cannot be used are macrolides (azithromycin), clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline, depending on the infection and the patient. For localized skin infections such as impetigo, topical mupirocin is not a penicillin.

Key fact: roughly 9 out of 10 people who believe they are allergic to penicillin are not, when tested with skin testing and supervised challenge. A wrong label leads to less effective, more expensive antibiotics. An allergist can confirm or rule out the allergy.
