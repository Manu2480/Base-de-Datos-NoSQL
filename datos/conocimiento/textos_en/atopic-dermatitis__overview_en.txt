Atopic dermatitis (eczema): overview, symptoms, treatment and precautions
Source: https://medlineplus.gov/spanish/ency/article/000853.htm; https://www.aeped.es/sites/default/files/documentos/11_dermatitis_atopica.pdf; https://extapps.childrenshospital.org/EFPEC/Home/Sheet/6263

Atopic dermatitis, also called atopic eczema, is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that causes dry, red, intensely itchy skin that flares up and then calms down. It often starts in infancy and may improve in adulthood, although in some people it persists or returns, frequently on the hands. It is caused by a weak skin barrier that loses water and lets irritants and allergens in, combined with an overactive immune response. It runs in families and is commonly linked to asthma and hay fever. It is not contagious.

Symptoms change with age. Infants have weeping, crusted patches on the cheeks, scalp and outer limbs. Children have patches in the creases of the elbows and knees. Adults tend to have thickened, leathery skin (lichenification) from chronic scratching. Itching is worse at night and disturbs sleep.

Treatment aims to control flares, since there is no cure. Daily moisturizers (emollients) and short lukewarm baths are the basis. During flares, topical corticosteroids are used: low potency such as 1% hydrocortisone for the face, skin folds and children, and medium potency such as betamethasone valerate 0.1% for the trunk and limbs. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are a second-line option from age 2. Severe disease may need phototherapy or biologic drugs such as dupilumab.

Precautions: people with atopic dermatitis are prone to bacterial infection (impetigo) and to widespread herpes infection (eczema herpeticum), which needs urgent antiviral treatment. Avoid strong steroids on the face and folds. Seek care if there are honey-colored crusts, pus, fever or clustered painful blisters.
