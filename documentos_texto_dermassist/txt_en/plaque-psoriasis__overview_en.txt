Plaque psoriasis: overview, symptoms, treatment and drug safety
Source: https://medlineplus.gov/spanish/ency/article/000434.htm; https://www.medlineplus.gov/spanish/psoriasis.html; Catálogo clínico del proyecto DermAssist (init/02-catalogo.js)

Plaque psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated skin disease in which skin cells multiply in days instead of weeks and pile up as thick, red, well-defined plaques covered with silvery-white scales. It typically appears on the elbows, knees, scalp and lower back, often symmetrically. It is not contagious. It tends to start between ages 10 and 35 and has a hereditary component. Flares can be triggered by infections, stress, dry skin, skin injury and some medicines. Some patients develop psoriatic arthritis, with joint pain and stiffness, and they carry a higher risk of cardiovascular disease and fatty liver.

Other signs include itching, cracked skin that bleeds, and nail changes such as pitting, thickening and separation from the nail bed.

Treatment is stepwise. Mild disease is treated with daily emollients and topical therapy: a potent corticosteroid combined with a vitamin D analogue such as calcipotriol. Clobetasol, a very potent steroid, is reserved for resistant plaques and is not used in children under 12 or for long periods on the face and folds. Narrow-band UVB phototherapy is used for wider disease. Moderate to severe disease needs systemic drugs: methotrexate, or biologics such as adalimumab (anti-TNF).

Safety: methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy, liver disease and kidney failure, and it interacts with trimetoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ibuprofen and penicillins such as dicloxacillin and amoxicillin-clavulanate, which raise its toxicity. Anti-TNF drugs are avoided in untreated tuberculosis or serious active infection and increase the risk of shingles and cellulitis. Regular blood tests are required with methotrexate.
