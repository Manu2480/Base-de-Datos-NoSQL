Basal cell carcinoma: the most common skin cancer
Source: https://medlineplus.gov/spanish/ency/article/000824.htm; https://www.mayoclinic.org/es/diseases-conditions/basal-cell-carcinoma/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20354193; https://www.mayoclinic.org/es/diseases-conditions/skin-cancer/symptoms-causes/syc-20377605; https://www.cancer.gov/espanol/tipos/piel/paciente/tratamiento-piel-pdq

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common skin cancer. It starts in the basal cells of the epidermis, grows slowly and rarely spreads, but if untreated it can invade and destroy nearby tissue, including cartilage and bone, especially on the face. The main cause is cumulative ultraviolet radiation from the sun and tanning beds, so it appears on sun-exposed areas such as the face, nose, ears, neck and scalp. Risk factors include fair skin, older age, previous sunburns or skin cancer, immunosuppression, prior radiation and genetic syndromes such as Gorlin syndrome.

Typical appearances are a pearly, translucent or pink nodule with visible tiny blood vessels, a sore that bleeds and crusts and never fully heals, a red scaly patch on the trunk, a flat scar-like waxy lesion, or a pigmented brown or bluish lesion that may be mistaken for melanoma. Squamous cell carcinoma, in contrast, is usually scaly, crusted and keratotic, grows faster and has a higher chance of spreading. Both can ulcerate. Diagnosis is by biopsy.

Treatment aims to remove the tumor completely. Options include surgical excision, Mohs micrographic surgery (highest cure rate and best tissue preservation, ideal on the face), curettage and electrodesiccation, cryotherapy, topical imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil for superficial tumors, photodynamic therapy, and radiotherapy. Advanced tumors may be treated with Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib), which are contraindicated in pregnancy. Cure rates exceed 95% with appropriate treatment. Patients need lifelong skin checks, daily sunscreen and sun avoidance.
